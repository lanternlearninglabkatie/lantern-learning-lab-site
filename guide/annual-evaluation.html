What is Florida's annual homeschool evaluation, and who can do it?

Florida requires every home education student to have an annual educational evaluation, filed with your district school superintendent. You choose the method and who does it — the law gives you five options, and a review by a certified teacher is one of them.

The five options the law allows

  1. A teacher you choose reviews the portfolio and talks with your child. The teacher must hold a valid regular Florida certificate to teach academic subjects at the elementary or secondary level.
  2. Your child takes a nationally normed achievement test, given by a certified teacher.
  3. Your child takes a state assessment test, given by a certified teacher, at a school-district-approved location.
  4. A licensed psychologist evaluates your child.
  5. Any other method you and your superintendent agree on.

Most families choose option 1. It is the least disruptive for a young child, it does not require buying or scheduling a standardized test, and it is the option the law itself describes first.

Who can do the evaluation

For option 1, the law only requires a valid regular Florida certificate to teach academic subjects at the elementary or secondary level — nothing about the teacher's own grade level or subject specialty. A certified teacher can evaluate a kindergartner or a high schooler, a struggling reader or a gifted mathematician.

What "evaluation" actually means

The law asks the teacher to document "the student's demonstration of educational progress at a level commensurate with her or his ability." That is relative to your child — not a fixed bar every student must clear. A thin portfolio from a child who is clearly moving forward is a pass. A thick one from a child who is not making progress is not.

The evaluation has two parts, both required:

  • Reviewing the portfolio — your log of activities and samples of your child's work
  • A conversation with your child — short, relaxed, about what they read and made this year

A portfolio review with no conversation does not satisfy the statute.

When it's due

The law says only that you file it annually — it sets no fixed date. Filing on the anniversary of your letter of intent is the safe, common habit, but it is not a legal deadline, and your district cannot invent one: Florida law says a district "may not further regulate ... or require documentation ... beyond the requirements of this section."

One detail that trips families up

Your letter of intent goes to the superintendent of the county where you, the parent, live. Your annual evaluation goes to the superintendent of the county where your student lives. Usually the same place — but if your family has moved, or you share custody across county lines, check both.

What happens if the evaluation doesn't show progress

The superintendent notifies you in writing. You then get one full year to provide remedial instruction, after which your child is re-evaluated. Continuing home education depends on showing progress at that point. This is not meant to be alarming — it almost never happens when a family is actually teaching — but it is the reason the conversation-with-your-child step exists, and the reason an evaluator who knows how to talk with kids matters.

What usually goes wrong

Families often assume they need to match their child's evaluator to a specific grade level, or worry that a struggling year will fail the evaluation. Neither is true the way people fear. The law measures progress against your child's own ability, not a scorecard, and a certified teacher can evaluate any grade.

The more common real problem is timing: families forget this is annual, not a one-time requirement when they filed their letter of intent, and scramble in May.
