What happens to your child's IEP when you start homeschooling?

Short answer: it stops applying. An IEP is a public school document, tied to public school enrollment — once your child is in a home education program, the district is no longer the one providing their education, and there is no homeschool equivalent that automatically takes its place.

Why it doesn't carry over

An IEP exists because federal law (IDEA) requires public schools to provide a free, appropriate public education to eligible students with disabilities. The services, accommodations, and goals in it are things the school is obligated to do. When your child leaves public school enrollment for home education, that obligation leaves with it — not because anyone revokes the IEP, but because the thing it was attached to no longer applies to your family.

Florida's home education statute (1002.41) doesn't require, reference, or provide any equivalent plan. There's no homeschool IEP, no state process that replaces it, and no form that recreates it. The gap is real, and it's simply not addressed by the law one way or the other.

What usually goes wrong

Parents tend to land on one of two opposite mistakes. Some assume the supports are just gone, and stop accommodating needs their child still has — not because the need disappeared, just because the paperwork that documented it did. Others go looking for a formal document or certification that "counts" as a homeschool IEP, assuming Florida must require something like it. It doesn't. Nothing is required. What's actually needed is simpler and more personal: understanding how your specific child learns, and building that into how you teach — which an IEP never fully captured anyway, since it was written for a classroom of thirty, not a homeschool of one.

What if my child goes back to public school later?

An old IEP generally stands for up to three years. In practice, when a child who qualifies enters a new district — including returning to public school after homeschooling — the IEP team meets, looks at whatever data is available, asks the parents questions, and writes a new IEP. It isn't simply reinstated exactly as it was before. The specifics can still vary by district, so this is worth a direct conversation with them if a return to public school is on the table.

What actually fills the gap

Nothing requires you to replace an IEP. But if your child benefited from accommodations at school — extra time, movement breaks, visual schedules, sensory supports — those needs don't go away just because the IEP did. This is exactly what an Individualized Learning Plan is for: not a legal document, not a replacement for an IEP, just a written plan for how your specific child learns best and what to build into your homeschool days, whatever curriculum you're using.

If what you're dealing with right now is more immediate — meltdowns, a child who won't start their work, a day that keeps falling apart — that's Meltdowns to Momentum, a faster, more focused version of the same idea.
